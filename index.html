NIV in the ED

A practical guide for Junior Doctors using the Hamilton T1 Ventilator

Visual NIV Physiology

Non-Invasive Ventilation forces air into the lungs. It improves gas exchange. It reduces the work of breathing. You must master the mechanics to treat patients safely.

Hypercapnic Failure (COPD) & Auto-PEEP

COPD patients suffer from airflow limitation. They trap gas inside their lungs. They cannot fully empty their lungs before the next breath begins. This creates intrinsic PEEP, known as auto-PEEP.

Auto-PEEP forces the patient to generate highly negative pleural pressures to initiate a breath. This fatigues the diaphragm. Applying EPAP externally counterbalances this auto-PEEP. It splints the small airways open during exhalation.

Small Airway

Collapsing early, trapping gas.

Adding Pressure Support increases the tidal volume. A larger tidal volume increases minute ventilation. Higher minute ventilation washes out retained carbon dioxide.

Hypoxic Failure (Pulmonary Oedema) & Recruitment

Left ventricular failure causes hydrostatic pressure to push fluid into the alveoli. Fluid-filled alveoli collapse. They cannot participate in gas exchange. This causes profound hypoxia.

Applying CPAP provides constant positive pressure. This splints collapsed alveoli open. It physically pushes fluid out of the alveolar space.

Alveolus

Collapsed and fluid-filled.

CPAP alters cardiac haemodynamics. Positive intrathoracic pressure decreases venous return. This reduces right ventricular preload. It decreases left ventricular transmural pressure, reducing afterload. The failing heart pumps more effectively.

Clinical Indications

Follow the BTS guidelines when prescribing NIV. Patient selection dictates success.

Type 2 Respiratory Failure (Hypercapnic)

Primary Indication: COPD Exacerbation.

Initiate BiPAP if the patient meets these criteria after 1 hour of optimal medical therapy. Optimal therapy includes bronchodilators, systemic steroids, and controlled oxygen.

  • pH < 7.35
  • PaCO2 > 6.5 kPa
  • Respiratory rate > 23 breaths per minute

Type 1 Respiratory Failure (Hypoxic)

Primary Indication: Cardiogenic Pulmonary Oedema.

  • Initiate CPAP if the patient remains hypoxic or in severe respiratory distress despite medical therapy (nitrates, diuretics, oxygen).
  • Start CPAP at 5 to 10 cmH2O.
  • Do not use BiPAP routinely for pulmonary oedema.

Contraindications

Do not use NIV if the patient has any of the following:

  • Impaired consciousness (GCS < 8).
  • Inability to protect their airway.
  • Severe facial trauma or burns.
  • Undrained pneumothorax.
  • Vomiting or bowel obstruction.

Step-by-Step Initial Setup (COPD Exacerbation)

  1. Turn on the Hamilton T1. Complete the pre-use checks.
  2. Select patient category and input height.
  3. Select Mode: Choose NIV-ST.
  4. Go to the Controls tab.
  5. Set PEEP to 5 cmH2O.
  6. Set Psupport to 10 cmH2O. This provides a total IPAP of 15 cmH2O.
  7. Set FiO2 to maintain SpO2 88-92%.
  8. Set backup Rate to 12 breaths per minute.

Monitoring and Troubleshooting

Assess your patient clinically. Interpret blood gases methodically.

ABG Schedule

  • Baseline: Before starting NIV.
  • 1 Hour: Do an ABG 1 hour after commencing NIV or any pressure change.
  • 4 Hours: Repeat ABG to ensure ongoing improvement.
Problem Action on Hamilton T1
High PaCO2 (Not clearing) Increase Psupport by 2 cmH2O to increase tidal volume.
Low PaO2 (Hypoxia) Increase FiO2. If still hypoxic, increase PEEP by 2 cmH2O. You must also increase Psupport to maintain the gap.
High Mask Leak (>20%) Reposition the mask. Check straps. Clear exhalation port.
Patient Asynchrony Check for leaks. Adjust Trigger Sensitivity. Coach the patient.

When to call Critical Care

  • pH dropping below 7.25 despite optimal NIV.
  • Falling GCS.
  • Haemodynamic instability.
