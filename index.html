      Circuit, interface and starting settings

      Mask type matters. The HAMILTON-T1 uses a coaxial or dual-limb circuit, and the patient breathes out through the ventilator's expiratory valve. Use a non-vented mask with a standard (non-vented) elbow: no exhalation port and no anti-asphyxia valve. Hamilton Medical's mask guide states that the standard elbow is "used with a coaxial or dual limb circuit on a Hamilton Medical ventilator" and that the anti-asphyxia (vented) elbows are "not to be used with ventilators". Vented masks belong with single-limb "ward NIV" machines that have no expiratory valve. Never put a non-vented mask on a single-limb circuit that has no exhalation port, because the patient will rebreathe CO2.

      Circuit layout (schematic)

      1. Expiratory valve set: seat the membrane with the metal plate facing up, then twist clockwise until it locks. Use the correct adult/paediatric valve.
      2. Inspiratory filter at the "To patient" port. The manual warns that exhaled gas can contaminate the ventilator if there is no inspiratory filter or patient-side HME/F.
      3. Circuit: coaxial or dual-limb, as stocked locally.
      4. Flow sensor in front of the patient connection. The end marked PATIENT faces the patient. Blue tube to the blue port, clear tube to the white port. Keep the tubes unkinked. A "Flip the flow sensor" alarm means it is reversed.
      5. Interface: a non-vented full face mask (BTS first-line interface) with a standard elbow.
      6. Then calibrate the flow sensor and run the Leak test with the complete circuit.

      The manual cautions that an extra component (such as an HMEF) between the flow sensor and the patient adds resistance. This limits the ventilator's ability to detect a disconnection at the patient. Set the low Pressure alarm and the volume alarms appropriately, and watch SpO2. Masks and HMEFs also add dead space. Check your trust SOP for filter/HMEF position in NIV

      Mask fitting and skin

      • Full face mask first (BTS/ICS Rec 2, Grade D). Keep a range of sizes, and make sure staff are trained to fit them.
      • Size with the manufacturer's sizing gauge. The cushion should sit from the bridge of the nose to just below the lower lip, without pressing on the eyes. mask IFU
      • Hold the mask on by hand while you start at low pressures and coach the patient. Then fit the headgear evenly.
      • Do not overtighten (BTS GPP). A small leak is better than a pressure injury. If leak is high, refit or change size or type rather than tightening.
      • Protect the nasal bridge according to local tissue-viability guidance. Check the skin regularly.
      • Nasal masks or pillows are for experienced practitioners when a full face mask is not tolerated. Mouth leak limits them.

      Humidification, oxygen and nebulisers

      • Humidification is not routinely required (BTS/ICS Rec 6). Consider heated humidification for mucosal dryness or thick, tenacious secretions. The T1 can work with a compatible heated humidifier and has an NIV temperature setting. Never use an HMEF and active humidification together. trust SOP
      • Oxygen is set with the Oxygen control (21–100%) through the internal blender. You do not need to entrain oxygen into the circuit. Target SpO2 88–92% in hypercapnic respiratory failure (BTS/ICS Rec 4).
      • Nebulisers: BTS advises giving nebulised drugs during breaks from NIV, or in-line if the patient depends on NIV. The T1 has a pneumatic nebuliser key (high-pressure O2 only) and supports Aerogen-type nebulisers. Check the expiratory valve and filter afterwards, because nebulised drug can affect them. trust SOP

      Starting settings and titration (hypercapnic respiratory failure)

      Translate IPAP/EPAP into T1 controls. On the T1, PEEP = EPAP, and ΔPsupport (NIV) or ΔPinsp (NIV-ST) is the pressure added on top of PEEP. So IPAP = PEEP + ΔP. For example, IPAP 20 with EPAP 5 is PEEP 5 and ΔP 15. If you raise PEEP, IPAP rises with it because the ΔP is kept. You do not need to add the same amount to ΔP to "keep the gap".

      A practical titration sequence adapt to local protocol

      1. Prescribe and document the plan, including the ceiling of treatment, before you start (BTS QS 5).
      2. Explain to the patient. Start with the mask held by hand at modest pressures, for example PEEP 5 and ΔP 10 (IPAP 15), or ΔP 15 (IPAP 20) if pH < 7.25. Set Oxygen for SpO2 88–92%.
      3. Over the first 10–30 min, increase ΔP in steps of 2–5 cmH2O towards IPAP 20–30, as tolerated. Look for chest and abdominal augmentation, a falling RR, synchrony and comfort.
      4. For persisting hypoxaemia not caused by sputum retention, consider increasing PEEP (BTS). Get a senior review if this does not work, and temporarily increase oxygen meanwhile.
      5. Fix leak, trigger and cycling problems before you call it NIV failure (BTS/ICS Table 3).
      6. Blood gas at 1 h and 4 h, and whenever the patient deteriorates (see Clinical).

      Cardiogenic pulmonary oedema (CPAP): use NIV mode with ΔPsupport = 0. The manual states that "the ventilator functions like a conventional CPAP system". PEEP is the CPAP level. Many protocols start around 5 and titrate towards about 10 cmH2O. local protocol

      Ventilation modes, triggering and cycling

      ED NIV mode

      NIV (noninvasive ventilation)

      Every breath is spontaneous (patient-triggered), with or without pressure support (ISO 19223: CSV-PS).

      • ΔPsupport (older software: Psupport): pressure above PEEP during inspiration. ΔPsupport 0 gives CPAP.
      • PEEP/CPAP, Oxygen
      • Synchronisation: Trigger, P-ramp, ETS, TI max
      • No set rate. If the patient stops breathing for the Apnea time, apnoea backup takes over if it is enabled (backup mode PCV+, or NIV-ST when the NIV-only option is active). If it is not enabled, a high-priority "Apnea" alarm sounds.

      Use for: CPAP (for example cardiogenic pulmonary oedema), or bilevel support in a patient with a reliable respiratory drive.

      ED NIV mode

      NIV-ST (spontaneous/timed)

      Every patient trigger gives a flow-cycled, pressure-supported breath. If the patient breathes slower than the set Rate, time-cycled mandatory breaths are delivered at that rate with the set TI (ISO 19223: SIMV-PC).

      • ΔPinsp (older software: Pinsp): pressure above PEEP for both spontaneous and mandatory breaths. Minimum 3 cmH2O in v3.1.x, so NIV-ST cannot deliver CPAP.
      • Rate (backup) and TI for mandatory breaths
      • PEEP, Oxygen, Trigger, P-ramp, ETS, TI max

      Use for: acute hypercapnic respiratory failure (COPD, OHS, neuromuscular or chest wall disease). This is the closest equivalent to "BiPAP S/T" with a backup rate, as BTS/ICS Figure 1 describes.

      Also on the T1

      HiFlowO2 (high-flow nasal oxygen)

      A continuous flow of air/oxygen through a nasal cannula: you set Flow (2–60 l/min for adults) and Oxygen. It is for patients who can breathe spontaneously and is not life-supporting. You can only select it in Standby, disconnection and apnea alarms are inactive, and Manual breath is disabled. The screen shows Oxygen and Flow trends and, with SpO2 on, the pleth (manual section 10.5). The manual warns that excessive flows could lead to barotrauma or pneumothorax, and that HiFlowO2 should not be used during intrahospital transport.

      Triggering, rise and cycling

      Trigger (inspiratory)

      Flow trigger, in l/min (v3.1.x adult range 0.5–20, default 5). A lower number is more sensitive. If it is too low you get auto-triggering, especially with leak. If it is too high you get missed efforts. Pink triangles on the pressure curve mark patient-triggered breaths. Some devices have an IntelliSync+ option (not in all markets).

      P-ramp (rise time)

      The time for pressure to rise to target, in ms. In NIV and NIV-ST it is limited to a maximum of 200 ms, and always to one-third of TI. A shorter ramp gives a faster, "punchier" breath, which is often preferred by breathless COPD patients. A longer ramp is gentler but can leave a hungry patient underserved.

      ETS (expiratory trigger sensitivity)

      Inspiration ends when flow falls to this percentage of peak inspiratory flow (range 5–80%, default 25%). Increasing ETS ends inspiration earlier. That gives more expiratory time, which helps in COPD, and copes with larger leaks (manual §7.6.4). With a big leak, flow may never fall to the ETS threshold, so TI max (0.5–3 s, default 1.5 s) ends the breath instead.

      IntelliTrig leak compensation

      In each breath, IntelliTrig measures the leak at the mask and adjusts the trigger mechanism. This reduces the influence of leak and changing breath patterns on the set trigger sensitivity (manual §7.6.7). In Hamilton Medical's internal testing, the HAMILTON-C1/T1/MR1 compensated up to 85 l/min inspiratory and up to 30 l/min expiratory leak, on specific software versions (Hamilton knowledge base). Compensation has limits. Leak still reduces the PEEP actually delivered and can cause auto-triggering, so fix the mask first.

      Physiology refresher: why EPAP and pressure support help

      Hypercapnic failure (for example AECOPD)

      Airflow limitation causes gas trapping and intrinsic PEEP. The patient must generate enough negative pressure to overcome it before a breath can be triggered, which is an inspiratory threshold load. EPAP/PEEP offsets intrinsic PEEP, which reduces the effort needed to trigger and improves comfort (BTS/ICS). Pressure support increases tidal volume and alveolar ventilation, which clears CO2 and unloads the respiratory muscles.

      Hypoxaemic failure (cardiogenic pulmonary oedema)

      CPAP recruits flooded, collapsed alveoli and improves oxygenation. Positive intrathoracic pressure reduces venous return (preload) and left ventricular transmural pressure (afterload), so a failing left ventricle can work more effectively. Watch for hypotension in preload-dependent patients.

      Alarms and troubleshooting

      Look at the patient first, not the screen. If the patient is deteriorating and you cannot quickly find the cause, remove NIV. Support with high-flow oxygen or bag-valve-mask as needed, call for senior help, and follow the escalation plan.

      A DOPES-style approach for NIV

      DDisplacement / disconnection

      Mask off or displaced, circuit or flow-sensor disconnection, big leak.

      OObstruction

      Upper airway (neck flexion, OHS), secretions, kinked or wet expiratory limb, blocked expiratory valve or filter.

      PPatient

      Pneumothorax, bronchospasm, fatigue, rising CO2 and drowsiness, vomiting or gastric distension, agitation.

      EEquipment

      Oxygen supply, power and battery, flow sensor, expiratory valve, circuit, settings and alarm limits.

      SSynchrony / stacking

      Missed triggers, auto-triggering, early or late cycling, intrinsic PEEP. Adjust trigger, ETS and PEEP.

      "DOPES" is a well-known mnemonic originally taught for intubated patients (Pneumothorax as "P", Stacked breaths as "S"). Here it is adapted for mask NIV.

      Clinical essentials (BTS/ICS 2016, BTS Quality Standards 2018)

      Indications

      • AECOPD: start NIV when pH < 7.35 and pCO2 > 6.5 kPa persist or develop despite optimal medical therapy (Rec 25, Grade A). Optimal therapy includes controlled oxygen, bronchodilators and steroids. BTS/ICS Figure 1 also lists RR > 23. Use it as supporting evidence of distress, not as an extra requirement.
      • Severe acidosis alone does not rule out a trial of NIV, provided it happens in an appropriate area with ready access to staff who can intubate safely (Rec 26). NIV must not delay escalation to invasive ventilation when that is more appropriate (Rec 27).
      • Advanced age alone should not rule out NIV (Rec 29).
      • Neuromuscular or chest wall disease: acute respiratory illness with RR > 20 (if usual VC < 1 L, even if pCO2 < 6.5), or pH < 7.35 and pCO2 > 6.5. Do not wait for acidosis. Get expert input early.
      • Obesity: pH < 7.35, pCO2 > 6.5 and RR > 23, or daytime pCO2 > 6.0 with somnolence. Higher pressures are often needed (for example IPAP > 30, EPAP > 8).
      • NIV is not indicated in asthma or pneumonia with hypercapnic failure. Refer to ICU to consider invasive ventilation (Figure 1; Rec 39).
      • Cardiogenic pulmonary oedema is outside the BTS/ICS hypercapnic guideline. NICE CG187 advises against routine NIV, but says to consider it without delay if there is severe dyspnoea and acidaemia. ERS/ATS 2017 suggests bilevel NIV or CPAP. local pathway

      Contraindications and cautions (BTS/ICS Figure 1)

      Absolute: severe facial deformity, facial burns, fixed upper airway obstruction.

      Relative: pH < 7.15 (or pH < 7.25 with an additional adverse feature), GCS < 8, confusion or agitation, cognitive impairment. Relative contraindications call for enhanced observation, consideration of HDU/ICU and an early decision on whether to continue NIV or move to invasive ventilation. They do not, on their own, mean NIV should be withheld.

      • Pneumothorax: drain an acute pneumothorax before NIV. If a small or chronic pneumothorax cannot be drained, proceed only with careful monitoring.
      • Vomiting: the key question is whether the mask can be removed quickly. Consider a nasogastric tube if there is marked abdominal distension.
      • Copious secretions increase the risk of failure, but NIV may still help.
      • Respiratory arrest or peri-arrest: intubate. A short NIV trial by an experienced operator is justified only in exceptional circumstances.

      The manufacturer's manual is more conservative. It lists no drive to breathe, airway obstruction, GI bleeding, intolerance of the interface and inability to cooperate or protect the airway as contraindications. It also requires the patient to be conscious and intubation to be possible at any time. Clinical decisions follow BTS/ICS and senior judgement. Be aware of the device labelling. trust SOP

      A fall in RR and an improved pH within 4 h predict NIV success (BTS/ICS). Persisting pH < 7.25 with RR > 35 makes failure likely. Capillary gases are acceptable. Transcutaneous CO2 can help.

      Monitoring and red flags

      • Continuous SpO2 (BTS GPP), with a target of 88–92%.
      • ECG monitoring if HR > 120, dysrhythmia or possible cardiomyopathy.
      • Serial observations and NEWS2 where it is used. A widely used schedule, from the 2008 RCP/BTS/ICS concise guideline, is every 15 min in the first hour, every 30 min from 1–4 h, then hourly from 4–12 h. local protocol
      • Observe chest wall movement, synchrony, accessory muscle use, RR, HR, SpO2, conscious level, comfort, mask fit and skin, and the leak readings.
      • Staffing: 1 nurse for every 2 NIV patients, especially in the first 24 h (BTS/ICS Box 3).
      Red flags (BTS/ICS Figure 1): pH < 7.25 on optimal NIV, RR persisting > 25, new confusion or patient distress. Actions: check synchronisation, mask fit and exhalation. Give physiotherapy and bronchodilators, consider an anxiolytic, and consider invasive ventilation if it is within the ceiling of treatment.

      Escalation and ceiling of care

      • Before starting, agree and document an individualised plan for what happens if NIV fails, involving the patient wherever possible (BTS GPP; QS 5). The plan should say whether NIV is the ceiling of treatment or whether invasive ventilation is appropriate.
      • Invasive ventilation should be considered for persistent or worsening acidosis despite optimised NIV (Rec 32). Intubate in respiratory arrest or peri-arrest unless there is rapid recovery (Rec 33).
      • Refer to ICU for: impending respiratory arrest, NIV failing to improve chest wall movement or reduce pCO2, inability to keep SaO2 above 85–88% on NIV, or a need for IV sedation, adverse features or a possible difficult intubation (Figure 1).
      • Prognostic tools can help discussions but predict poorly for individuals. Clinicians tend to underestimate survival with invasive ventilation in AECOPD (Recs 36–37).
      • When NIV is the ceiling of care and the patient is distressed, sedation or anxiolysis for symptom control is appropriate (Rec 9). IV morphine 2.5–5 mg, with or without a benzodiazepine, may help an agitated patient tolerate NIV (GPP). Use close monitoring, and give infusions only in HDU or ICU.

      Weaning and documentation

      • Maximise time on NIV in the first 24 h, as far as tolerance and complications allow (BTS GPP).
      • Over the next 2–3 days, taper NIV during the day according to the self-ventilating pCO2, before stopping it overnight.
      • Stop NIV when pH and pCO2 have normalised and the patient has generally improved (Rec 31). Reduce pressures or the backup rate gradually as the patient recovers.

      Document

      • Indication and the pre-NIV blood gas, with times
      • The NIV prescription: mode, PEEP, ΔP (and resulting IPAP), Rate and TI, oxygen and SpO2 target, mask type and size
      • The escalation plan and ceiling of care, and who agreed it
      • Every setting change, with the reason and the time. Blood gas times and results. Observations.
      • Skin checks, breaks for drinks, nebulisers and mouth care, and patient tolerance
      • Review times (4 h and 14 h, QS 5)

      NCEPOD's 2017 report Inspiring Change found that care could have been improved in many patients receiving acute NIV, including blood gases taken too infrequently and gaps in prescribing and escalation planning.

      Nurse focus

      At the bedside

      • Check the ventilator: preop checks passed (green ticks, valid date), Leak test run with the full circuit, battery charged, O2 connected, mains plugged in.
      • Confirm the circuit: non-vented mask, standard elbow, flow sensor the right way round, filter fitted.
      • Coach the patient: explain, hold the mask on first, sit them upright, and give them a call bell.
      • Set and check alarm limits with the prescriber, especially the low Pressure limit near the set pressure, so that a disconnection alarms.
      • Observations to the agreed schedule (commonly every 15 min in the first hour). Watch SpO2 88–92%, RR, HR, conscious level, synchrony and leak readings.
      • Skin: check the nasal bridge and pressure points. Avoid overtightening. Pad according to tissue-viability guidance.
      • Planned breaks for drinks, mouth care and nebulisers, with controlled oxygen on and SpO2 monitored. Keep breaks short in the first 24 h.
      • Watch for gastric distension or vomiting, and for agitation, which may be asynchrony.
      • Escalate immediately if there is a red flag, a blood gas that has not improved, drowsiness, or an alarm you cannot fix.
      Doctor focus

      Decisions

      • Confirm the indication after optimal medical therapy. Find and treat the reversible cause (infection, pneumothorax, oversedation, pulmonary oedema, effusion).
      • Look for contraindications and adverse features. Decide the location (HDU or ICU if high-risk) and involve the ICU team early.
      • Agree and document the escalation plan and ceiling of treatment before starting. Talk to the patient and family.
      • Write a clear prescription in T1 terms: PEEP, ΔP (IPAP = PEEP + ΔP), Rate/TI in NIV-ST, Oxygen, SpO2 target and alarm limits.
      • Titrate actively in the first 30 min towards IPAP 20–30 as tolerated. Do not leave the patient on starting pressures.
      • Review the 1 h blood gas yourself. If it has not improved, check the technical issues (BTS/ICS Table 3) and then escalate within 30 min (QS 6).
      • Know when to stop: persisting acidosis, exhaustion, falling GCS or haemodynamic instability. Intubate if that is within the ceiling of care, or move to palliation.

      Starting invasive ventilation on the T1

      For the first hour after intubation in the ED, before ICU takes over. Use your trust's post-intubation checklist and local ventilation guidance alongside this.

      Set-up sequence

      1. Preoperational check in Standby: leak test and flow sensor calibration (System > Tests & calib). It is the same check as for NIV: see the walk-through in the NIV tab.
      2. Patient group, sex and height in the Standby window. The T1 calculates ideal body weight (IBW) from these, and IBW sets the starting Vt, the APRV and ASV starting values and the ASV targets. Measure height; do not guess from weight.
      3. Choose a mode (Modes, then Confirm), review the controls in the Controls window that opens, then Confirm again.
      4. Set the alarm limits (Alarms > Limits). The high Pressure limit also sets Plimit (10 cmH2O below it), the highest pressure the T1 will use in the adaptive modes. The blue line on the pressure waveform shows Plimit.
      5. Start ventilation, check chest movement, SpO2, waveforms and blood pressure, confirm tube position (waveform capnography), then take a blood gas.

      A common starting point

      Mode(S)CMV+ (volume-targeted, adaptive pressure) or PCV+. Most passive, paralysed patients are started on a fully controlled mode.
      Vt6–8 ml/kg IBW. Use 6 ml/kg in ARDS (ARDSNet; FICM/ICS guideline: under 6 ml/kg).
      RateOften 14–20 b/min. Match the patient's pre-intubation minute ventilation if they were compensating for a metabolic acidosis. Use a low rate in asthma and COPD.
      PEEP5 cmH2O to start; higher in hypoxaemia (see Oxygenation).
      OxygenStart high after intubation, then titrate down to the SpO2 target.
      I:E1:2 is typical; 1:3 or longer when air trapping is a risk.

      These are widely taught starting values, not a protocol. Check your local guidance.

      Pplateau ≤ 30 cmH2O. The ARDS Network trial used Vt 6 ml/kg predicted body weight with plateau pressure kept at or below 30 cmH2O, and showed lower mortality than 12 ml/kg. The T1 shows Pplateau and Driving pressure (Monitoring > General 1) for mandatory, time-cycled breaths.

      The T1 times mandatory breaths with Rate and TI, and shows either I:E or TI depending on the breath timing chosen in Configuration (manual 7.1.1). The trainer shows I:E in (S)CMV+ and PCV+ and TI in the SIMV modes.

      Volume target or pressure target?

      In (S)CMV+, SIMV+ and VS you set the volume and the T1 finds the pressure. If the lungs get stiffer, pressure rises; if it reaches Plimit, Vt falls short and the Pressure limitation alarm appears.

      In PCV+ and PSIMV+ you set the pressure. If the lungs get stiffer or the tube blocks, Vt falls, so the Vt and ExpMinVol low alarms are your warning.

      Apnea backup

      In the modes where the patient must trigger (SIMV+, PSIMV+, SPONT, VS, DuoPAP, APRV), set Apnea time and keep Apnea backup on (Controls > Apnea). If the patient stops breathing, the T1 switches to backup ventilation and shows "Apnea ventilation". A sedated, paralysed patient on SPONT will be ventilated only by backup breaths. Try it in the simulator.

      ASV and the bilevel modes

      Intelligent ventilation

      ASV (Adaptive Support Ventilation)

      • You set %MinVol: the target minute ventilation as a percentage of normal for the patient's IBW (100% is normal; the trainer uses 0.1 l/kg/min). Also set PEEP, Oxygen and the high Pressure limit.
      • ASV chooses the rate and Vt it expects to need the least work of breathing, then adjusts pressure and mandatory rate breath by breath. Passive patients get mandatory breaths; active patients get supported breaths.
      • Lung-protective rules (ASV quick guide): Vt at least 4.4 ml/kg IBW (twice the estimated dead space); maximum rate set by expiratory time constant (at least 1 × RCexp inspiration and 2 × RCexp expiration); pressure never above Plimit; target volume no more than 1.5 × the Vt high limit.
      • ASV Graph (Graphics): the green curve is the target minute volume, the dashed box is the safety frame, the circle is the target and the cross is the patient's actual breathing.
      • Adjusting: in a passive patient with a high pCO2, increase %MinVol in steps of 10–20%; in an active patient in distress, steps of 20% with 2–5 minutes between them (ASV quick guide).
      Bilevel

      DuoPAP and APRV

      DuoPAP switches between P high and PEEP/CPAP. The manual describes it as resembling PCV+ at conventional settings with no spontaneous breathing, looking more like PSIMV+ at low rates, and like APRV when T high takes up almost the whole cycle. ΔPsupport is set above PEEP, so it only helps breaths at P high when PEEP + ΔPsupport is above P high.

      APRV holds P high for a long T high and releases briefly to P low. For a patient with no spontaneous breathing it is similar to pressure-controlled inverse ratio ventilation. The first time you select it, the T1 proposes P high 20, P low 5, T high 5.4 s and T low 0.6 s for IBW over 60 kg. A short T low leaves air in the lungs on purpose, so watch AutoPEEP.

      APRV is a specialist mode. In the ED, use it only under ICU guidance.

      Oxygenation, CO2 and air trapping

      Oxygenation

      Driven by Oxygen and mean airway pressure (mostly PEEP). In hypoxaemia, raise PEEP as well as Oxygen, watching blood pressure and Pplateau. Common targets: SpO2 88–95% in ARDS (ARDSNet), 94–98% for most other acutely ill patients (BTS oxygen guideline), 88–92% if at risk of hypercapnic failure.

      CO2

      Driven by alveolar minute ventilation: rate × (Vt − dead space). If you lower Vt to protect the lungs, raise the rate to keep minute ventilation. In ARDS a modestly raised pCO2 is usually accepted rather than giving large tidal volumes.

      Air trapping (AutoPEEP)

      If expiration is too short for the lungs to empty, the next breath starts on top of trapped air. Expiratory flow does not return to zero before the next breath, AutoPEEP rises (Monitoring), delivered Vt falls in pressure-controlled breaths, and blood pressure can fall. Treat with a lower rate and a longer expiratory time (I:E 1:3 or 1:4). In severe asthma, accept a raised pCO2 to allow this.

      Troubleshooting a ventilated patient

      If the patient is deteriorating and the cause is not obvious: call for help, disconnect from the ventilator and hand-ventilate with 100% oxygen through a bag and the tube, with waveform capnography attached. Then work through DOPES.

      DOPES

      • Displacement of the tube: check capnography, tube length at the teeth, chest movement.
      • Obstruction: kinked tube, secretions (pass a suction catheter), biting.
      • Pneumothorax: unequal chest movement, rising pressures, falling blood pressure.
      • Equipment: circuit disconnection or leak, flow sensor, oxygen supply, ventilator failure.
      • Stacked breaths: air trapping (see AutoPEEP above). Briefly disconnecting lets trapped air out.

      T1 alarms worth knowing

      • Pressure high: the breath is cut short at the high Pressure limit. Think obstruction, coughing, biting, bronchospasm, pneumothorax.
      • Pressure limitation: inspiratory pressure has been held at Plimit. In the adaptive modes this means the T1 needs more than Plimit to reach Vt. Lungs stiffer? Tube partly blocked? Vt too high?
      • Disconnection on patient side (invasive modes): expired volume is much less than inspired volume.
      • Vt low / ExpMinVol low: leak, disconnection, or stiffer lungs in a pressure-controlled mode.
      • Inspiratory volume limitation: delivered Vt is more than 1.5 times the Vt high alarm limit. The T1 ends the breath, and the APV modes lower the pressure for the next breath by 3 cmH2O.
      • Apnea / Apnea ventilation: no breaths within the Apnea time; backup ventilation starts if it is enabled.

      In the simulator, open the alarm buffer and touch an alarm for its help text. Use Inject a problem to practise.

      CPR ventilation

      Touch Modes, then the red CPR button, review the controls and Confirm. You can start it from Standby or during ventilation (manual section 10.9).

      • Default mode APVcmv: Rate 10, I:E 1:5, PEEP 5, Plimit 45, Oxygen 100%, Vt 6 ml/kg IBW (PCV+ can be configured instead, with ΔPcontrol 15).
      • Flow trigger is Off and P-ramp is fixed at 50 ms, so chest compressions do not trigger breaths.
      • The ExpMinVol, fTotal and Vt alarm limits go to their minimum and maximum. The CPR ON alarm and the CPR Timer show while it is on.
      • To stop: select and confirm another mode (alarm limits return to their previous settings), or enter Standby. "Start CPR" restarts it from Standby.

      Follow Resuscitation Council UK guidance for ventilation during CPR; this section only describes the T1 function.

      Practise on the simulator

      Choose an Intubated patient in the Patient list beside the simulator. Each starts in Standby on (S)CMV+ at typical post-intubation settings, with its own guided tasks.

      ARDS

      Hypoxaemic, stiff lungs

      1. Start ventilation and open Monitoring > General 1. Note the high driving pressure at 8 ml/kg.
      2. Lower Vt to about 6 ml/kg IBW and raise the rate to keep minute ventilation.
      3. Raise PEEP and bring Oxygen down towards 40%, keeping SpO2 88–95%.
      4. Take a blood gas. Then try PCV+ with the same pressures and see how Vt changes.
      Severe asthma

      Air trapping

      1. Start ventilation at Rate 18, I:E 1:2. Watch the expiratory flow fail to reach zero, AutoPEEP rise and the blood pressure fall.
      2. Lower the rate and lengthen expiration (I:E 1:4) until AutoPEEP is below 2 cmH2O.
      3. Take a blood gas: a raised pCO2 is expected and usually accepted.

      The physiology is a simplified single-compartment model for teaching. It does not model haemodynamics in detail, harm from excessive PEEP, or recruitment over time.
