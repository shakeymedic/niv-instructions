NIV in the ED

A practical guide for Junior Doctors using the Hamilton T1 Ventilator

NIV Physiology

Non-Invasive Ventilation provides positive pressure to the airways. This improves gas exchange and reduces the work of breathing. You must understand the underlying mechanics to treat your patients effectively.

Hypercapnic Failure Mechanics (COPD)

COPD patients suffer from airflow limitation and gas trapping. They cannot fully empty their lungs before the next breath begins. This creates intrinsic PEEP (auto-PEEP).

Auto-PEEP forces the patient to generate highly negative pleural pressures just to initiate a breath. This causes severe diaphragm fatigue. Applying EPAP externally counterbalances this auto-PEEP. It splints the small airways open during exhalation. This reduces the inspiratory effort required.

Adding Psupport increases the tidal volume. A larger tidal volume increases minute ventilation. Higher minute ventilation washes out retained carbon dioxide.

Hypoxic Failure Mechanics (Cardiogenic Pulmonary Oedema)

Left ventricular failure causes hydrostatic pressure to push fluid into the alveoli. Fluid-filled alveoli cannot participate in gas exchange. This creates an intrapulmonary shunt and profound hypoxia.

Applying CPAP provides a constant positive pressure. This pressure splints collapsed alveoli open. It increases functional residual capacity and physically pushes fluid out of the alveolar space.

Crucially, CPAP alters cardiac haemodynamics. Positive intrathoracic pressure decreases venous return to the right heart. This reduces right ventricular preload. It also decreases left ventricular transmural pressure. This reduces left ventricular afterload. Your patient's failing heart will pump much more effectively against a lower afterload.

Key Terms Summary

  • CPAP: Delivers a single, constant pressure. It increases functional residual capacity and improves oxygenation.
  • BiPAP: Delivers two varying levels of pressure.
  • EPAP: The lower pressure during exhalation. It maintains alveolar recruitment and overcomes auto-PEEP.
  • IPAP: The higher pressure during inhalation. It provides a pressure boost to increase tidal volume and clear carbon dioxide.

Clinical Indications & Guidelines

Follow the BTS guidelines when prescribing NIV in the Emergency Department. Patient selection dictates success.

Type 2 Respiratory Failure (Hypercapnic)

Primary Indication: COPD Exacerbation.

Initiate BiPAP if the patient meets these criteria after 1 hour of optimal medical therapy. Optimal therapy includes bronchodilators, systemic steroids, and controlled oxygen titrated to 88-92%.

  • pH < 7.35
  • PaCO2 > 6.5 kPa
  • Respiratory rate > 23 breaths per minute

You can also use BiPAP for neuromuscular disease (like Motor Neurone Disease) or chest wall deformities presenting with acute hypercapnic respiratory failure.

Type 1 Respiratory Failure (Hypoxic)

Primary Indication: Cardiogenic Pulmonary Oedema.

  • Initiate CPAP if the patient remains hypoxic or in severe respiratory distress despite medical therapy. Medical therapy includes nitrates, diuretics, and high-flow oxygen.
  • Start CPAP at 5 to 10 cmH2O.
  • Do not use BiPAP routinely for pulmonary oedema unless concurrent hypercapnia is present.

Contraindications

Do not use NIV if the patient has any of the following:

  • Impaired consciousness (GCS < 8); an exception is hypercapnic encephalopathy where a short trial is acceptable under close supervision.
  • Inability to protect their airway or clear secretions.
  • Severe facial trauma, burns, or recent upper airway surgery.
  • Undrained pneumothorax.
  • Vomiting or bowel obstruction.
  • Life-threatening hypoxaemia requiring immediate intubation.

Setting up the Hamilton T1

The Hamilton T1 calculates pressure settings differently from standard ward BiPAP machines. You must understand the additive nature of the settings to avoid under-ventilating your patient.

IPAP = PEEP + Psupport

Key Settings Translation

  • PEEP on the Hamilton equals EPAP.
  • Psupport (in NIV mode) or Pinsp (in NIV-ST mode) equals the additional pressure given above PEEP.
  • You cannot input the IPAP directly. You must calculate the Psupport required to reach your target IPAP.

Advanced Settings: Triggers

The ventilator must synchronise with the patient. It does this using flow triggers.

  • Inspiratory Trigger: This determines how much effort the patient needs to initiate a breath. Leave it at the default setting initially. If the patient struggles to trigger breaths, lower the threshold.
  • ETS (Expiratory Trigger Sensitivity): This dictates when the machine cycles from inspiration back to expiration. It is a percentage of peak inspiratory flow. COPD patients need a long time to exhale. Set the ETS higher (e.g. 40-50%) for COPD patients. This cuts off the inspiratory pressure sooner and gives them more time to empty their lungs.

Step-by-Step Initial Setup (COPD Exacerbation)

  1. Turn on the Hamilton T1 and complete the pre-use checks.
  2. Select your patient category (Adult) and input their height.
  3. Select the mode: Choose NIV (spontaneous breaths only) or NIV-ST (sets a backup respiratory rate).
  4. Go to the Controls tab.
  5. Set PEEP to 4 or 5 cmH2O (This is your EPAP).
  6. Set Psupport (or Pinsp) to 10 cmH2O. This provides a total IPAP of 15 cmH2O.
  7. Set FiO2 to maintain target saturations. This is usually 88-92% for COPD.
  8. If using NIV-ST, set a backup Rate. Aim for 12 to 15 breaths per minute.

Monitoring and Titration

NIV requires close monitoring. Assess your patient clinically and interpret their blood gases methodically.

Complications to Monitor

  • Hypotension: Positive intrathoracic pressure reduces venous return. Check blood pressure frequently after initiating therapy.
  • Barotrauma: High pressures can cause pneumothoraces. Examine for unequal breath sounds.
  • Skin Breakdown: Tight masks cause nasal bridge necrosis. Apply protective dressings early.
  • Gastric Distension: Air enters the stomach at higher pressures. This increases vomiting risk.

ABG Schedule

  • Baseline: Before starting NIV.
  • 1 Hour: Do an ABG 1 hour after commencing NIV or 1 hour after any pressure change.
  • 4 Hours: Do another ABG to ensure ongoing improvement.
Problem Action on Hamilton T1
High PaCO2 (Not clearing) Increase Psupport by 2 to 3 cmH2O to increase tidal volume. Monitor patient tolerance.
Low PaO2 (Hypoxia) Increase FiO2 first. If still hypoxic, increase PEEP by 1 to 2 cmH2O. Note: If you increase PEEP, you must also increase Psupport to maintain the same tidal volume gap.
High Mask Leak (>20%) Reposition the mask. Check the straps. Ensure the exhalation port is clear.
Patient Asynchrony Check leak. Adjust Inspiratory trigger or ETS. Coach the patient. Consider mild sedation only with senior critical care input.

When to call ICU

Escalate to critical care immediately if the patient develops:

  • pH dropping below 7.25 despite optimal NIV.
  • Falling GCS.
  • Haemodynamic instability.
  • Inability to tolerate the mask.

Clinical Case Scenarios

Apply your physiological knowledge to these common ED presentations.

Scenario 1: COPD Exacerbation

Presentation: A 68-year-old man presents with worsening breathlessness. He has a background of severe COPD. He sits on the edge of the bed using accessory muscles. His respiratory rate is 32 breaths per minute. An ABG on 2L oxygen via nasal cannula shows:

  • pH: 7.26
  • PaCO2: 10.2 kPa
  • PaO2: 7.1 kPa
  • Bicarbonate: 34 mmol/L

Pre-NIV Pathophysiology: He has life-threatening acute-on-chronic type 2 respiratory failure. Severe airway inflammation causes premature airway collapse during exhalation. This traps gas in the alveoli. The trapped gas generates an intrinsic positive end-expiratory pressure (auto-PEEP) of approximately +6 cmH2O. His diaphragm is pushed flat and operates at a severe mechanical disadvantage. To draw a new breath, he must generate a negative intrathoracic pressure of -8 cmH2O just to overcome the auto-PEEP. This causes extreme fatigue. His tidal volumes drop to 200ml. This shallow breathing vastly increases dead space ventilation and causes PaCO2 to spike. Alveolar hypoventilation leads to profound V/Q mismatching.

Hamilton T1 Setup:

  • Mode: NIV-ST
  • PEEP: 5 cmH2O.
  • Psupport: 12 cmH2O.
  • FiO2: Titrate to achieve SpO2 88-92%.

Post-NIV Pathophysiology: Applying 5 cmH2O of PEEP externally balances his internal auto-PEEP. His inspiratory effort required to trigger a breath drops from -8 cmH2O to just -1 cmH2O. This provides immediate relief to his fatigued diaphragm. The Psupport of 12 cmH2O delivers a targeted pressure boost during inspiration. His tidal volume increases from 200ml to 450ml. This massive increase in alveolar ventilation clears the retained CO2. Fresh oxygen reaches previously under-ventilated alveoli. This rapidly corrects the V/Q mismatch.

Next Steps: Reassess clinically in 15 minutes. Repeat the ABG in 1 hour. Adjust Psupport based on the new PaCO2 and pH.

Scenario 2: Cardiogenic Pulmonary Oedema

Presentation: A 75-year-old woman presents with sudden onset severe dyspnoea. She has a history of ischaemic heart disease. She sits upright and sweats profusely. You hear widespread fine crackles on auscultation. Her respiratory rate is 36 breaths per minute. Her SpO2 is 82% on 15L oxygen via a non-rebreathe mask. Her blood pressure is 180/100 mmHg.

Pre-NIV Pathophysiology: She has severe hypoxic type 1 respiratory failure. Acute left ventricular failure causes high left ventricular end-diastolic pressure (LVEDP). This pressure transmits backwards into the pulmonary circulation. Hydrostatic forces push transudate fluid from the capillaries into the interstitial spaces and alveoli. Fluid-filled alveoli collapse. They receive pulmonary blood flow but zero ventilation. This creates a massive right-to-left intrapulmonary shunt. The V/Q ratio in these affected lung units drops to zero. Lung compliance falls sharply. She breathes rapidly to maintain minute ventilation against stiff lungs.

Hamilton T1 Setup:

  • Mode: NIV
  • PEEP: 10 cmH2O.
  • Psupport: 0 cmH2O.
  • FiO2: 100% initially.

Post-NIV Pathophysiology: Applying a constant CPAP of 10 cmH2O physically forces fluid out of the alveoli and back into the pulmonary interstitium. The pressure splints collapsed alveoli open. This restores her functional residual capacity (FRC). Ventilation returns to the previously shunted areas. V/Q matching improves dramatically. Oxygen diffuses normally again. Furthermore, the positive intrathoracic pressure compresses the vena cava. This reduces venous return and lowers right ventricular preload. The positive pressure also surrounds the failing left ventricle. This lowers the transmural pressure required to eject blood. Left ventricular afterload falls. Her cardiac output improves.

Next Steps: Monitor her blood pressure closely. The reduced venous return can cause sudden hypotension. Continue medical therapy including IV nitrates. Wean the FiO2 as her oxygenation improves.
